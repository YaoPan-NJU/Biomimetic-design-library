# Catechol pH-Dependent Bidentate Coordination

> Rule ID: CM-001 | Confidence: 0.9 | Last validated: 2026-06-05 (LLM draft, pending literature validation)

## Core Claim

In the pH 3-7 range, catechol hydroxyl groups progressively deprotonate, forming increasingly stable bidentate coordination bonds with transition metal ions.

## Detailed Explanation

Catechol (1,2-dihydroxybenzene) possesses two adjacent hydroxyl groups on the aromatic ring. These hydroxyl groups have acid dissociation constants pKa1 ~ 9.2 and pKa2 ~ 12.6 in the free (uncoordinated) state. However, the presence of metal ions dramatically shifts these equilibria through Le Chatelier's principle -- as metal ions coordinate with deprotonated oxygens, they pull the equilibrium toward deprotonation at much lower pH values.

The coordination proceeds in a stepwise fashion:

1. **pH 3-5**: The first hydroxyl deprotonates upon metal binding, forming a monodentate complex. The stability constant is moderate (log K1 ~ 5-8 for most divalent metals).

2. **pH 5-7**: The second hydroxyl deprotonates, completing the bidentate chelate ring (a stable 5-membered metallocycle). The overall stability constant increases significantly (log beta2 ~ 10-20 depending on the metal).

3. **pH 7-9**: The bidentate complex is fully formed and most stable. For metals like Fe3+, tris-catecholate complexes can form with log beta3 > 35.

The bidentate coordination mode is particularly effective because:
- The 5-membered chelate ring has minimal ring strain
- The two oxygen donors are held in an ideal cis-geometry by the aromatic backbone
- The aromatic ring provides rigidity, reducing the entropic cost of complex formation

In mussel foot proteins (mfps), the DOPA residue provides catechol functionality that coordinates with metal ions in seawater (pH ~8.1). In polydopamine (PDA), residual catechol groups from incomplete oxidation of dopamine serve the same coordination function. Plant tannins contain multiple gallic acid or catechin units with catechol-like moieties.

## Positive Example

Mussel-inspired polydopamine coatings achieve Hg2+ adsorption capacities of 200-400 mg/g at pH 5-7, where catechol groups are partially to fully deprotonated. The bidentate coordination mode is confirmed by XPS showing characteristic Hg-O binding energy shifts and by FTIR showing C-O stretching frequency changes upon metal binding.

## Counter-Example / Boundary Condition

At very high catechol density on rigid substrates (e.g., densely grafted catechol on silica nanoparticles), steric crowding may prevent all catechol groups from achieving bidentate geometry simultaneously. In such cases, some catechol groups remain monodentate even at optimal pH, reducing the effective coordination capacity below theoretical predictions.

## Applicable Prototypes

- **mussel-foot-adhesion**: DOPA residues in mussel foot proteins coordinate with metal ions via catechol bidentate binding; natural seawater pH (~8.1) is near optimal
- **polydopamine-coating**: Residual catechol groups in PDA films provide metal coordination sites; maximum adsorption typically observed at pH 5-7
- **plant-tannin**: Condensed and hydrolyzable tannins contain multiple catechol-type dihydroxyl groups; metal chelation capacity increases with pH in the 3-7 range

## Literature Sources
